\title{Reconciling Gao--Harshaw--S\"avje--Wang with Park's Design-Based Exposure-Map Tests}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\section{Purpose}

Gao, Harshaw, S\"avje, and Wang (2026, henceforth GHSW) prove an impossibility theorem for specification testing of exposure-mapping models. Park (2026) proposes design-based GMM tests and estimators for parametric exposure maps. At first glance, the two claims may appear inconsistent: one paper states that specification testing of exposure mappings is impossible, while the other constructs specification tests for exposure maps. The apparent conflict disappears once the null hypotheses, alternative spaces, and auxiliary conditions are kept distinct.

The central distinction is the following. GHSW rule out \emph{uniformly informative testing against unrestricted refinements of exposure mappings}. Park's procedure is not a uniformly consistent test against that alternative space. It tests a finite collection of design-implied moment restrictions within a maintained, structured exposure family, and its consistency statements require identification and regularity conditions on the induced moment criterion. Thus Park's procedure can be informative for the specific misspecifications targeted by its moments, but it should not be interpreted as validating an exposure map against all possible refinements.

\section{Exposure-map definitions}

\subsection{GHSW: exposure mappings as equivalence relations on assignments}

GHSW consider a finite experiment with intervention space $\Omega$. For a binary-treatment experiment, $\Omega = \{0,1\}^n$, but the formal setup is more general. Unit $i$ has a potential-outcome function
\[
    y_i : \Omega \to \mathbb R,
\]
and the full potential-outcome schedule is $y=(y_1,\ldots,y_n)$.

An exposure mapping for unit $i$ is a function
\[
    h_i : \Omega \to \Delta_i,
\]
where $\Delta_i$ is a finite set of exposure labels. The exposure model generated by $h=(h_1,\ldots,h_n)$ is
\[
    \mathcal M_h
    = \Bigl\{y : y_i(z)=y_i(z') \text{ for all } i \text{ and all } z,z' \in \Omega
    \text{ such that } h_i(z)=h_i(z'), \ \|y\|_\infty \le 1 \Bigr\}.
\]
Equivalently, $y\in \mathcal M_h$ if and only if, for every unit $i$, there exists a function
\[
    \widetilde y_i : \Delta_i \to \mathbb R
\]
such that $y_i(z)=\widetilde y_i(h_i(z))$ for every assignment $z$. The exposure mapping is therefore not a parametric index by itself; it is a partition of the assignment space into cells inside which the unit's potential outcome is restricted to be constant.

This definition implies that exposure models are ordered by refinement. If $h_i^1$ is a refinement of $h_i^0$, then the finer exposure $h_i^1(z)$ determines the coarser exposure $h_i^0(z)$. Formally, there is a map $m_i:\Delta_i^1\to\Delta_i^0$ such that
\[
    h_i^0(z)=m_i(h_i^1(z)).
\]
In this case, the model generated by $h^0$ is nested in the model generated by $h^1$:
\[
    \mathcal M_{h^0} \subseteq \mathcal M_{h^1}.
\]
The finer model permits outcomes to vary across refined exposure cells that the coarser model pools together.

\subsection{Park: parameterized exposure indices and exposure sufficiency}

Park starts from a known assignment design $D_n$ for the treatment vector $W$. The assignment may be binary, multi-valued, or continuous. For each assignment $w$, unit $i$ has a potential outcome $Y_i(w)$, and the realized outcome is $Y_i=Y_i(W)$.

Park's exposure map is a parameterized function
\[
    g : \mathcal W \times \Theta \to \mathbb R^k,
\]
possibly depending on unit-level covariates $X_i$. The paper writes
\[
    g_i(W;\theta) := g(W;X_i,\theta),
\]
where $\theta\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb R^p$ indexes a feature of the exposure construction, such as a radius or a decay parameter. Park's maintained exposure-sufficiency null is
\[
    H_0^{P}:
    \exists \theta_0\in\Theta \text{ and functions } \widetilde Y_i:\mathbb R^k\to\mathbb R
    \text{ such that }
    Y_i(w)=\widetilde Y_i(g(w;X_i,\theta_0))
    \quad \forall i,w .
\]
This is the same conceptual restriction as in GHSW: conditional on the correct exposure value, the remaining details of the assignment are irrelevant for the unit's outcome. The difference is that Park places this restriction inside a parameterized family $\{g(\cdot;\theta):\theta\in\Theta\}$ and uses the design to estimate or test $\theta$.

When $g_i(\cdot;\theta)$ takes finitely many values, Park's exposure-sufficiency condition can be written as membership in a GHSW-style exposure model. For fixed $\theta$, define $h_i^\theta(w)=g_i(w;\theta)$ after relabeling the finite range of $g_i$. Then
\[
    \{Y_i(w)=\widetilde Y_i(g_i(w;\theta))\ \forall i,w\}
    \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
    Y \in \mathcal M_{h^\theta}.
\]
Thus the primitive notion of correctness is not fundamentally different. What differs is the inferential question.

\section{Small but important discrepancies}

\paragraph{Finite versus continuous exposures.}
GHSW formulate the main impossibility result for exposure mappings with finite exposure sets $\Delta_i$. Park allows real-valued exposure indices, including continuous radii and smooth kernel parameters. For finite grids or discrete network radii, Park's setting can be embedded directly into the GHSW framework. For continuous scalar rings or smooth decay maps, the exposure family is not literally the same finite-label object, although a sufficiently fine grid approximation brings the two frameworks close in practice.

\paragraph{Fixed exposure model versus unknown parameter.}
GHSW test a fixed null exposure model $\mathcal M_0=\mathcal M_{h^0}$ against a larger exposure model $\mathcal M_1=\mathcal M_{h^1}$. Park's null is composite: there exists some $\theta_0\in\Theta$ such that exposure sufficiency holds. In model notation, Park's null is closer to
\[
    \mathcal M_P = \bigcup_{\theta\in\Theta} \mathcal M_{g(\theta)}.
\]
The estimation of $\theta_0$ is part of the problem. Therefore the Park procedure is not simply a test of one fixed partition against a finer partition.

\paragraph{The meaning of separation.}
GHSW use a separation functional on potential-outcome schedules. Their separation measures how much the outcome function varies within coarse exposure cells when the alternative permits those cells to be split. In simplified notation, if $e^0$ is a coarse exposure and $s_i(e^0)$ is the set of finer exposures that refine it, their separation is of the form
\[
    s(y)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{e^0\in\Delta_i^0}
    \sup_{e^1,e^{1\prime}\in s_i(e^0)}
    \bigl(\beta_{i,e^1}-\beta_{i,e^{1\prime}}\bigr)^2 .
\]
This is separation in the outcome schedule itself.

Park's identification conditions use a different type of separation. For regular GMM consistency, the population criterion
\[
    Q(\theta)=\mu(\theta)'\Lambda\mu(\theta)
\]
must have a unique zero at $\theta_0$, or, in a finite nested class, insufficient classes must have a positive profile-criterion gap from the sufficient set. This is separation in the \emph{chosen moment criterion}, not separation in the full potential-outcome schedule. GHSW show that even large outcome-schedule separation does not by itself guarantee the existence of a uniformly powerful test. Park obtains consistency only after imposing that the misspecification is visible through the particular design-based moments used in the criterion.

\paragraph{Alternative space.}
GHSW's alternative is deliberately broad. It contains all bounded potential-outcome functions compatible with the finer exposure mapping, including adversarial functions chosen to be empirically indistinguishable from null functions under the realized-assignment observation. Park's overidentification test is not designed for that full alternative space. Its population alternative is the set of data-generating schedules for which the selected residualized moments cannot be made zero for any $\theta\in\Theta$.

\paragraph{Auxiliary large-sample conditions.}
GHSW's impossibility theorem is finite-sample and minimax. It holds without smoothness, moment-differentiability, affinity-set laws of large numbers, or central limit theorems. Park's positive asymptotic results require additional structure: deterministic stabilization of the moment means, a uniform law of large numbers, identification of the limiting criterion, dependence restrictions through affinity sets, and regularity conditions for asymptotic normality. For ring exposures, Park also requires shell or grid regularity to control jumps in the cutoff path. These conditions are not consequences of exposure sufficiency alone.

\section{GHSW's theorem and proof}

GHSW consider a testing procedure $(D,\varphi)$, where $D$ is the experimental design and $\varphi(z,y(z))\in\{0,1\}$ is a test based on the realized assignment and observed outcomes. For a null model $\mathcal M_0$ and a separated alternative $\mathcal M_1(\delta)$, define the overall testing error
\[
    R(D,\varphi,\delta)
    = \sup_{y\in\mathcal M_0} E_D[\varphi(Z,y(Z))]
    + \sup_{y\in\mathcal M_1(\delta)} E_D[1-\varphi(Z,y(Z))].
\]
The minimax testing error is
\[
    R^*(\delta)=\inf_{D,\varphi} R(D,\varphi,\delta).
\]
Their Theorem 3.1 states that, for any nested exposure models $\mathcal M_0\subseteq\mathcal M_1$ and any non-trivial separation value $\delta$,
\[
    R^*(\delta)=1.
\]
This means that no test is uniformly better, in the minimax sense, than a procedure that ignores the data and randomizes rejection.

The proof is a least-favorable-mixture argument. GHSW construct a mixture $\Pi_0$ supported on the null and a mixture $\Pi_1$ supported on the separated alternative. A general testing lower bound gives
\[
    R^*(\delta)
    \geq
    1-\sup_D E_{Z\sim D}
    d_{TV}\{Q_0(Z),Q_1(Z)\},
\]
where $Q_k(z)$ is the distribution of the observed outcome vector $y(z)$ under mixture $\Pi_k$.

For the general nested exposure case, write null functions as
\[
    y_i(z)=\sum_{e^0\in\Delta_i^0}\alpha_{i,e^0}1\{h_i^0(z)=e^0\}
\]
and alternative functions as
\[
    y_i(z)=\sum_{e^1\in\Delta_i^1}\beta_{i,e^1}1\{h_i^1(z)=e^1\}.
\]
Under $\Pi_0$, the coefficients $\alpha_{i,e^0}$ are independent Rademacher variables. Under $\Pi_1$, coefficients are also Rademacher at the level observed under any realized assignment, but within each split set of the refinement they are chosen so that at least two refined exposures receive opposite signs. Hence the alternative functions are separated from the null, because outcomes vary across refined exposure cells inside a coarse exposure cell.

However, for every realized assignment $z$, the observed vector
\[
    y(z)=(y_1(z),\ldots,y_n(z))
\]
has the same distribution under $\Pi_0$ and $\Pi_1$: its coordinates are independent Rademacher variables in both cases. Therefore
\[
    d_{TV}\{Q_0(z),Q_1(z)\}=0
    \quad \text{for every } z\in\Omega.
\]
The lower bound then gives $R^*(\delta)\ge1$. Since the trivial non-informative risk level is attainable, $R^*(\delta)=1$.

The key point is not that every specific misspecification is undetectable. Rather, for any exposure-mapping test aimed at a broad refinement alternative, there exist misspecifications in that alternative that are observationally indistinguishable from the null in the design-based experiment.

\section{Park's overidentification test}

Park's main moment construction starts from exposure sufficiency. Fix a candidate $\theta$ and a design function $\psi(W)$. Define the design-side residual
\[
    R_{i,\theta}\psi(W)
    = \psi(W)-E[\psi(W)\mid g(W;X_i,\theta)],
\]
where the conditional expectation is taken under the known design $D_n$. If exposure sufficiency holds at $\theta_0$, then $Y_i$ is measurable with respect to $g(W;X_i,\theta_0)$. Therefore, for any integrable outcome transformation $\phi$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
    E\bigl[\phi(Y_i)R_{i,\theta_0}\psi(W)\bigr]
    &= E\Bigl[ E\{\phi(Y_i)R_{i,\theta_0}\psi(W)
        \mid g(W;X_i,\theta_0)\}\Bigr] \\
    &= E\Bigl[ \phi(Y_i)
        E\{R_{i,\theta_0}\psi(W)\mid g(W;X_i,\theta_0)\}\Bigr] \\
    &=0.
\end{aligned}
\]
The equality is an implication of the null; it is not, by itself, a power statement against all alternatives.

Given a finite dictionary $\{(\phi_m,\psi_m):m=1,\ldots,M\}$, Park defines moment contributions
\[
    \eta_{m,i,n}(\theta)=\phi_m(Y_i)R_{i,\theta}\psi_m(W),
    \qquad
    \eta_{m,n}(\theta)=\frac1{N_n}\sum_{i\in U_n}\eta_{m,i,n}(\theta),
\]
stacks them into $\eta_n(\theta)$, and minimizes
\[
    Q_n(\theta)=\eta_n(\theta)'\Lambda_n\eta_n(\theta).
\]
When $M>\dim(\theta)$, the minimized criterion yields a GMM overidentification statistic. Under the maintained exposure family, the chosen moment dictionary, and the large-sample regularity assumptions, the statistic has the usual design-based limiting interpretation.

This test has the same limitation as any finite-moment specification test: it detects misspecification only through the moments included in $\eta_n(\theta)$. If there exists a misspecified exposure schedule for which
\[
    E[\phi_m(Y_i)R_{i,\theta}\psi_m(W)]=0
    \quad\text{for all included }m\text{ and some }\theta,
\]
then the J-test has no population reason to reject that misspecification. Increasing the dictionary can enlarge the set of detectable alternatives, but GHSW show that no procedure based only on the realized experimental data can be uniformly powerful against unrestricted exposure-map refinements.

\section{Why the results do not contradict each other}

There would be a contradiction if Park claimed to construct a test whose minimax error tends to zero for every non-trivially separated refinement alternative considered by GHSW. Park does not make that claim. The Park test is conditional on a maintained exposure family, a chosen class of moments, and identification/regularity conditions ensuring that the misspecification is reflected in the population GMM criterion.

The formal relationship is as follows. Let $\mathcal H_P=\{g(\cdot;\theta):\theta\in\Theta\}$ be Park's maintained exposure family, and let $\mathcal A_{\eta}$ denote the alternatives detectable by the selected moments:
\[
    \mathcal A_{\eta}
    = \Bigl\{Y:\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_Y(\theta)'\Lambda\mu_Y(\theta)>0\Bigr\},
\]
where $\mu_Y(\theta)$ is the population vector of Park moments under the potential-outcome schedule $Y$. Park's consistency logic applies to alternatives in $\mathcal A_{\eta}$, provided the relevant uniform convergence and dependence conditions hold. GHSW's alternative is much larger: it includes all bounded potential-outcome schedules in a refined exposure model that are separated from the coarser model, including schedules for which the observed-data distribution can be matched by a null mixture. These least-favorable alternatives need not lie in $\mathcal A_\eta$ for Park's chosen moments, and GHSW show that some such alternatives must evade any test if the alternative space remains unrestricted.

The two results are therefore complementary. GHSW delimit what cannot be learned from randomization alone without restricting the alternative. Park supplies one way to impose economically motivated restrictions: specify a parametric exposure family, choose design functions that target deviations of interest, and use the design to estimate and test the induced moment restrictions. The resulting J-test is a diagnostic for the maintained specification, not a universal certificate of exposure-map correctness.

\section{Implications for how Park's test should be described}

The comparison suggests two wording restrictions for Park's paper. First, a non-rejection should not be described as ``validating'' the exposure map in an unrestricted sense. A more precise statement is that the selected moments do not reject the maintained exposure family. Second, rejections should be interpreted as evidence that the proposed exposure family fails the tested design-implied restrictions, not as a complete characterization of the true spillover structure.

Similarly, the term ``separation'' should be used carefully. In GHSW, separation is a property of the potential-outcome function relative to a coarser exposure partition. In Park, the relevant separation conditions are identification assumptions on the population moment criterion or fixed profile gaps in finite nested classes. These are mathematically different assumptions. The latter can hold in applications even though the former impossibility theorem remains true for unrestricted refinement alternatives.

\section{Diagnostic conclusion}

The papers do not have conflicting results. They answer different questions. GHSW ask whether one can design a specification test that uniformly distinguishes a null exposure model from all sufficiently separated potential-outcome schedules in a more expressive exposure model. Their answer is no. Park asks whether, within a structured exposure family and a chosen set of design-based moments, the known assignment mechanism can be used to estimate exposure parameters and test the implied orthogonality restrictions. Under additional identification, dependence, and regularity assumptions, the answer can be yes.

Thus Park's overidentification test is legitimate as a restricted diagnostic. It becomes inconsistent with GHSW only if it is interpreted as an unrestricted validation test against all possible exposure-map refinements. The appropriate interpretation is narrower: rejection indicates failure of the maintained exposure family along the tested moment directions, while non-rejection means only that the data do not reveal such a failure through those moments.
